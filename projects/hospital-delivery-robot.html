Hospital Autonomous Secure Delivery Robot

A delivery robot for moving supplies between hospital departments. It drives itself down a corridor, stops before it hits anything, and only opens its cargo box for an authorized RFID card.

My roleElectrical architecture, PCB shield, control integration
  • Altium Designer
  • Arduino C++
  • PCB assembly
  • RFID
  • Power rails

BriefThe problem

Hospitals move medication, samples and supplies between departments all day. The work is repetitive and time-sensitive, and it carries a chain-of-custody requirement: the cargo can only go to the right person.

Our brief was a vehicle that drives a corridor on its own, refuses to run into anything, and will not release its cargo without authorization.

PCB designOff the breadboard, onto a custom shield

I designed the electrical architecture as a single-layer Altium PCB shield that stacks directly onto an Arduino Uno. Every subsystem gets its own labelled header: 5 V power, RFID, ultrasonic, motor driver, Bluetooth, temperature sensor, servo and LCD, plus an onboard push button with a 10 kΩ pull-down.

Labelling every connector on the silkscreen meant the harness could only go together one way, which removed a whole class of wiring mistakes during integration.

PowerTwo rails from one battery

  • One 11.1 V LiPo feeds two buck converters: a 5.1 V logic rail and a 7.9 V motor rail.
  • Keeping the rails separate holds motor switching noise off the sensors and the I²C bus.
  • Stable ultrasonic readings are what made the obstacle stop safe to rely on.

Control loopSensing and actuation

Five sensors and four actuator systems run in one microcontroller control loop:

  • HC-SR04 ultrasonic measures range ahead and stops the robot inside 20 cm.
  • RC522 RFID reader unlocks the cargo compartment only for an authorized tag.
  • HC-05 Bluetooth carries commands and telemetry.
  • DHT11 logs temperature and humidity inside the cargo box in transit.
  • A 16×2 LCD shows status, two DC motors run through an L298N, and a servo drives the latch.

IntegrationBuild and debug

I specified the components, drew the schematics and kept the full bill of materials. I then hand-soldered and assembled the shield, and used a multimeter to trace each wiring and integration fault back to its root cause until every subsystem test passed.
